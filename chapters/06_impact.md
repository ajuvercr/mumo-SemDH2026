# Implications for the Digital Humanities Community {#sec:impact}

It is important to acknowledge that environmental sensor data is, strictly speaking, operational infrastructure data rather than cultural or humanistic content. Museum monitoring captures physical conditions—temperature, humidity, light—for preservation management, not for humanistic interpretation or scholarly inquiry in the traditional sense. The contribution of MuMo to Digital Humanities is therefore not a new dataset for humanistic scholarship, but rather a tested architectural pattern and a set of practical lessons that are transferable to DH settings with similar structural characteristics.

Many Digital Humanities projects share the conditions that motivated MuMo: data that is collected continuously over long periods, embedded in local institutional infrastructures, and subject to distributed governance—whether sensor readings, annotations, evolving metadata, or digitized collections. In practice, such data often remains siloed within project-specific systems, limiting reuse, comparability, and shareability across institutional boundaries. Digital Humanities researchers have increasingly emphasized that the sustainability of digital resources is not only a technical issue but also an institutional and socio-technical one, shaped by funding horizons, maintenance practices, and governance constraints [@tucker2022facing; @fenlon2020sustaining].

The experience reported in this paper suggests that **dataspace-oriented approaches** are well suited to such settings. By allowing data to remain under the control of the institution that produces it while enabling integration at the point of use, dataspaces provide a viable alternative to centralized platforms. This pattern—decentralized publication, semantic alignment, and governance-aware access control—is not specific to sensor data and could be applied to DH collaborations involving multiple partners with heterogeneous governance structures and long-lived legacy systems.

The use of **semantic representations as infrastructural connectors**, rather than as expressive modeling exercises, further supports interoperability in constrained environments. Lightweight semantic alignment enables independently managed datasets to be combined, extended, and reinterpreted over time, without requiring uniform tooling or deep semantic expertise from end users.

Together, these observations point toward a pragmatic path for DH infrastructures that emphasizes decentralization, semantic interoperability, and governance-aware design over comprehensive but fragile integration solutions. While MuMo itself addresses an operational concern in cultural heritage, the architectural lessons are relevant wherever long-running, distributed data must be shared under institutional constraints.
